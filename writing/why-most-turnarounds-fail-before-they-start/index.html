Why Most Turnarounds Fail Before They Start

The operational mistakes that doom organizational recovery efforts, and the four-question diagnostic Sean M. Hogan uses instead.

By Sean M. Hogan · October 17, 2026

Most turnarounds do not fail in execution. They fail in the first ninety days, when leaders mistake the symptoms they can see for the problem they need to solve.

When an organization is in trouble, the pressure to act is enormous. Boards want a plan. Staff want reassurance. Donors, customers and partners want to know the ship is being steered. So new leaders do what feels responsible: they cut costs, reorganize the chart, and launch a strategic plan. Each of those moves can be right. Made before the diagnosis is complete, each of them can also lock in the very dysfunction that caused the crisis.

Three mistakes I see most often

Treating the budget as the diagnosis. A deficit is a result, not a cause. Cutting to balance this year's numbers without understanding why revenue and cost drifted apart usually produces a smaller version of the same organization, with the same failure mode intact and less capacity to fix it.

Fixing the org chart before the operating system. Reorganizations are visible and fast, which makes them tempting. But if decisions, information and accountability do not flow cleanly between people, moving the boxes only changes who is frustrated.

Trusting the reports. In a struggling organization, the dashboards are often part of the problem. Data lives in disconnected systems, definitions differ by department, and nobody fully trusts the numbers. A leader who steers by those reports is steering by a broken compass.

The diagnostic I use instead

Before committing to a recovery plan, I work through four questions, in this order.

  1. Where does the money actually come from and go? Not the budget categories, but the true unit economics: which programs, partners or products create value, and which consume it.
  2. How do decisions really get made? Who decides, with what information, and how long does it take? Bottlenecks here explain more failures than strategy ever does.
  3. Which systems hold the truth? Map where critical data lives, how it moves, and where it breaks. Fragmented systems create fragmented organizations.
  4. What does the culture reward? People repeat what gets noticed. If the culture rewards heroics over process, or loyalty over candor, no plan will survive contact with it.

Only after those answers are clear does the plan get written. It is usually simpler than anyone expected, because it addresses causes rather than symptoms.

What this looked like at Alpha USA

When I stepped into concurrent COO, CFO and CPO responsibilities at Alpha USA, the temptation was to start cutting. Instead, we started by understanding how value moved through a national network of partner organizations. That diagnosis shaped a financial turnaround and an operating model that let the partner network grow from roughly 5,000 to roughly 10,000 organizations.

The takeaway

Speed matters in a turnaround, but speed in the wrong direction is the most expensive mistake an organization can make. The leaders who succeed are the ones disciplined enough to diagnose before they prescribe, and honest enough to let the diagnosis change their plan.
