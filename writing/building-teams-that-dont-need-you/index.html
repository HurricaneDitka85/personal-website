Building Teams That Don't Need You

What counseling psychology teaches about organizational dependency, and how leaders build cultures that outlast their founders.

By Sean M. Hogan · October 17, 2026

Before I led organizations, I studied how people grow. My graduate training in counseling psychology was about individuals and families, not companies. But the longer I lead, the more I see the same dynamics at organizational scale. Healthy systems produce people who can function on their own. Unhealthy ones produce dependency.

Dependency looks like loyalty

In family systems, an over-functioning member often has an under-functioning counterpart. One person carries the load, so others never learn to. Both roles feel normal from the inside, and both people are stuck.

Organizations reproduce this pattern constantly. A leader who rescues every project trains the team to wait to be rescued. A manager who rewrites every deliverable teaches people that their first draft does not matter. The culture that results feels loyal and close-knit. It is also brittle.

Three signs a team depends on you too much

  1. Problems arrive as questions, not proposals. People bring you the issue and wait, rather than bringing a recommendation.
  2. Work slows when you travel. Progress tracks your availability more closely than anyone's capacity.
  3. Feedback flows one way. People rarely challenge your thinking, which means you are no longer getting their best thinking.

What builds independence instead

Differentiation, not distance. In counseling, differentiation means staying connected while remaining a distinct self. For a leader, it means caring deeply about the team's success without absorbing their responsibilities. You can be close and still say, "That one is yours."

Clarity before autonomy. People cannot own what they cannot see. Clear goals, decision rights and shared data are the preconditions for independent judgment. Autonomy without clarity is just abandonment.

Tolerating the learning curve. Delegated work will be done differently, and sometimes worse, at first. Leaders who step back in at the first mistake teach the team that ownership is temporary.

Naming the pattern out loud. Teams often know the dependency exists. Saying it plainly, and committing together to change it, turns an unspoken dynamic into a shared goal.

Why this matters for growth

An organization can only scale as far as its leaders' judgment is distributed. When decisions, knowledge and relationships are held by a few people, growth stalls at their limits. When they are held by a team of capable, independent leaders, growth compounds.

The takeaway

The highest compliment a team can pay its leader is not "we couldn't have done it without you." It is "we've got this." Building that kind of team is slower in the short run and dramatically faster in the long run. It is also the only kind of culture that outlasts its founders.
